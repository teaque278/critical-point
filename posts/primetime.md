> There used to be a pillory in every civilized town. Now there’s a television in every home.
>
> Chris Hansen, *Primetime*

I went to watch Primetime with my boyfriend, totally ready to watch Chris Hansen get all the praise he deserved for exposing the filth in our society. I couldn’t think there could be *anything* wrong with broadcasting sick child predators on television.

I walked out of the theatre with the heartbreaking realisation that there is a fundamental problem with doing good for an audience. 

Primetime follows the story of Chris Hansen, an NBC journalist and rise and fall of his renowned show, *To Catch a Predator.*

## Vigilante Journalism

The movie opens with Chris and his crew setting up a trap for the predator. Camera rolls, mics are in place and the predator walks in. The ‘fake’ child leads him to the kitchen, asks him to wait there for her and disappears upstairs. Minutes later, Chris emerges, reciting the most depraved texts that the predator had sent the child. Chris makes his way closer to the predator, making him cower before Chris’ imposing presence.

*You see how this looks right?*

Fear flushes into the predator’s face as cameras and boom mics suddenly surround him like weapons. He tries to run out the front door but is met with a swarm of policemen who tackle him down and handcuff him roughly. Cameramen follow as the predator cries and begs, trying to reason with the policemen as he’s being taken away.

All of this seems like an appropriate punishment for a child predator. They must be publicly shamed. Everyone should know their faces.

*Then what’s the problem?*

## The Intent

To Catch a Predator aired in 2008, when the internet was the wild west. There were little to no guardrails on the content or chat spaces an individual could access. It was the emergence of a breeding ground for predators. 

This show used the same lawless internet to catch these predators. Publicly humiliating them on television was the *'greater good'* that this show gave back to the world. That’s until they were moved up to a primetime slot to compete with *Lost.*

To Catch a Predator was exposing filth, no doubt. But the filth had to get dirtier and more disgusting for them to keep their primetime position. Their focus was thus shifting from catching a predator to catching the *most depraved one.*

## The Audience

The need to grab the audience’s attention is what pushed the show’s creators to look for the worse of the worst predators out there in America.

Why, as an audience, are we so drawn to the kind of entertainment this show offered? I can draw parallels with ***MTV’s Catfish*** that made me feel the same kind of way that this show did. I call it the ***high-horse watching***, or watching from sitting on a high-horse, in simple words. The idea of watching moral justice prevailing is exhilarating, but if I tune in every week on the same channel, same time, to watch this moral justice being enforced in the most humiliating way, at some point it stops being about justice. It turns inward. It becomes about this odd contentment that one gets from watching people being humiliated, and the fact that these ones *‘deserved’* it lets viewers avoid any responsibility for their interests or as I called it, the high-horse watching.

Just before the film’s concluding climax, Pattinson says to Decoy Dan’s character *“There used to be a pillory in every civilised town. Now there is a television in every home”*

He admits that the show and its ratings were all tied to one thing. Public humiliation.

Everything boiled down to sensationalization of the depravity of these predators. That’s what catapulted the views & ratings. The show was clocking nearly 7 million viewers at its peak. *Seven. Million.* All of them tuning in to see how this predator is more fucked up than the previous one.

Nobody watches if you catch the same kind of predators again. You might be saving a dozen children but *To Catch a Predator* was never about that. In the end, it was a TV show, with a producer whose money was on line and a journalist who had to fight tooth and nail to get his ratings up. 

That is what became the fundamental problem with doing good for an audience. 

You stop *doing good.*
